Aftershock Machine

A digital Rube Goldberg machine. You shake a web page, a real earthquake answers, and the shaking travels to another place in the world that happens to have the same name.

The project

Rube Goldberg drew machines that use many silly steps to do one small task. The fun is in watching the chain, not in the result. The Web works in a similar way. One small action in the browser can start many hidden requests between servers far away.

Aftershock Machine makes that hidden chain visible. You "make an earthquake" by scrolling hard on a seismograph. How fast you fill the meter decides how big your quake is: a wild, fast shake is like a magnitude 7, a slow one is like a magnitude 5. The page then finds a real earthquake from the past year with that same magnitude. The exact millisecond your meter filled picks which one, so no two shakes land on the same quake.

Then the machine asks a simple question: what is this place called, and is there another place in the world with the same name? Many towns share names. There are Victorias in Canada, the Seychelles and Texas. There are Santa Cruzes in Bolivia, California and the Philippines. The machine looks up every name the epicenter goes by, finds a namesake far away, and draws a red fault line between the two. The ground shook in one Victoria. The other Victoria felt nothing. Only their names connect them.

Before you start, the map already shows the seafloor, the edges of the tectonic plates in red, and every earthquake of magnitude 4.5 or more from the past 12 months as a black dot (about seven thousand of them). The chain also collects pictures: a satellite view of the ground that shook, photos people took near the epicenter, the name itself, photos of the namesake, and a satellite view of it. At the end, they are lined up as a film strip. Every picture can be clicked to see it larger.

The mechanics

Questions from the brief

What kind of data is passed around?

It starts as the speed of your hand and becomes a magnitude. The magnitude finds a real earthquake: a time, a depth and a pair of coordinates. The coordinates become place names. The names are then used as a key to search the whole world for other places that share them, which gives a new pair of coordinates. So the chain goes from motion, to a number, to a place, to a word, and back to a different place. Along the way, every place also becomes pictures: satellite views and photos tied to its location.

Which services are chained together?

StepServiceWhat it does here
01USGS Earthquake CatalogGives every earthquake of magnitude 4.5 or more from the past year. The machine keeps the ones that match your shake and picks one.
02BigDataCloud + Wikimedia CommonsTurns the epicenter coordinates into place names, and finds photos people took within 10 km.
03USGS place label + BigDataCloudCollects every name for the spot: nearest town, district, province, region.
04Open-Meteo Geocoding (GeoNames data)Searches the world for other places with exactly the same name, at least 150 km away. The quake's depth picks one. If a name has no twin, it tries the next name.
05Wikipedia + Wikimedia CommonsFinds the article and photos for the namesake.
06Leaflet + Esri map and satellite imageryDraws the "fault line" between the two places that share a name, and shows the namesake from above.

What does the trigger look like?

A seismograph at the top of the page. Scrolling on it (or holding the button on a phone) makes the line shake, and the whole page shakes with it. If you stop, the energy slowly drains away, so you have to keep shaking. When the meter is full, your small fake earthquake "wakes up" a real one of the same size. Fill it in about 1.5 seconds and you get a magnitude 7; take 8 seconds and you get a magnitude 4.7.

What is the end result?

A red dashed line on a map between two places that have nothing in common except a name, and one sentence, for example: "The ground shook near Victoria. Far away, there is another Victoria in the Seychelles, 9,000 km away. It felt nothing." Under it is a film strip of every picture the chain passed through. The result is useless. What matters is watching every step happen: the ball moves down the track, each box lights up, and the raw data is shown as it is passed along.
